#### EXTENDED RESPONSE

Albert Einstein, the 20th century physicist, famously said that "Science without religion is lame, religion without science is blind." Over the last two-hundred years, as scientific hypothesis and religious beliefs have grown more informed, the schism between the two has seemingly grown more divided. Though, there are numerous ways in which the complementarity between science and religion can be supported.

To interpret this essay question, I ask how religious beliefs and scientific hypotheses/discoveries can accept caveats to their interaction in order to aid the complementarity of the two, and how this openness is a hard task. This essay will focus on 3 scientific typologies for the discussion of religion.

1. **Psychological & sociological analysis of religion** — as a social tool for unity.


2. **Natural theology** — the study of the natural world in search for God, which incorporates religion into the gaps left unanswered by scientific hypothesis.


3. **The conflict between modern science and religion, specifically over divine intervention**.



Ultimately, this essay will focus on a Western-Christian view of religion, although this thesis, as a whole, can provide a point of view for a broad topic. Similarly, scientific hypothesis and scientific testing will focus on the last two-thousand years.

Ultimately, a willingness to accept caveats to scientific hypotheses and religious beliefs can illustrate complementarity between the two, although this is a hard task.

In the 19th century, sociologist Emile Durkheim proposed a unified definition for religion. Durkheim argues that religion is a system of beliefs and practices under a "collective conscience" that binds a community using reasons of providing stability to a community, especially in times of rapid and severe change.

This assumption is reinforced by 20th-century philosopher John Hick, who proposed that religion is the "transformation from self-centredness to reality-centredness." Hence, salvation is a turn away from private ambition to keep the "collective conscience" alive. Hick reasserts the hypothesis that religion provides reason for the survival of the species, regardless of whether the beliefs are based in truth or not.

---

### Page 7

In this way, religion can be seen as a tool for civic order. Using Durkheim's and Hick's definition of religion, a scientific argument can be made for the complementarity of science and religion.

Stephen J. Gould, an American biologist, proposed a system where science and religion are considered "Non-Overlapping Magisteria" (NOMA). Under NOMA, science is left to deal with empirical facts of nature, while religion is able to provide answers to questions of morality and ethics.

This is especially apparent to the debate over complementarity as science is unable to empirically prove a sense of right and wrong. Thus, complementarity can be achieved over the scientific hypothesis of surface-level definition of religion, seemingly keeping religious morality and ethics intact.

---

### Page 8

Although, neuroscientist and philosopher Sam Harris rejects the idea of NOMA, under the assumption that it concedes the whole of morality to religion. In a 2023 interview, Harris argued that if someone were to lose their religious beliefs, they would lose their sense of morality.

John Lennox, the contemporary mathematician and theologian, argued contra Harris, explaining that a sense of morality (specifically in Western culture) stems from the habituation of religious beliefs, such as Christianity. Lennox argued that Harris formed his sense of morality via the religious society it lives (or lived) in.

For example, the central Christian sense of sexual immorality can be traced back to 1 Corinthians 6:18. Likewise, the central Christian value of humility can be traced to Philippians 2:3–4: "Do nothing out of selfish ambition or vain conceit. Rather, in humility value others above yourself." As religion expanded, so too did these values spread to the societies they reached.

---

### Page 9

This hypothesis formed by sociologists such as Durkheim is also apparent in pre-Christian societies such as Ancient Greece. This hyper-religious society had moral codes startingly different from that of modern Western society today. They often glorified polygamy, sexual transgression, and folklore, such as in Homer's literature *The Iliad*.

Therefore, an argument can be made to illustrate the complementarity between science and religion over maintaining religious beliefs about morality and ethics. Though, this definition of religion and its complementarity with science comes with a caveat.

Joseph Ratzinger, who later became Pope Benedict XVI, criticized this hypothesis, claiming that religion's ability to provide answers lies in its ability to speak to the deepest questions of human existence, such as the origins of life or the meaning of life. Benedict argues that when religion is reduced to a societal construct or civic tool, it loses the value to provide these profound answers to societal issues.

---

### Page 10

This, therefore, is the caveat of the psychological and sociological relationship between science and religion. To achieve complementarity, Benedict argues that religion was rendered unsustainable and in the end allowed the mindset that sought to enable pagan religions to fizzle out.

Thus, this complimentary attempt by Gould, Durkheim, Lennox, and Hick can only be achieved under a willingness to allow religion to be reduced to a social construct, which is unsustainable.

Is there, therefore, a way for the profound answering ability explained by Benedict to be kept while still offering complementarity between science and religion?

"Natural Theology" — the study of the natural world in search for God — is seemingly able to incorporate religious beliefs about origins alongside scientific hypotheses. Through scientific discovery, various truths discovered about the universe have been able to point to the existence of a divinity who created the universe.

### Page 11

20th-century Quantum Physicist and Theologian, John Polkinghorne, provides two arguments, based on scientific hypothesis, that provide evidence for a creator God in his book *Science and Creation*.

Polkinghorne's first argument, the "intelligibility of the world argument," hinges on the basis that because we are bound under two laws — order and chaos — Polkinghorne reasons that the universe is uniform and that we can rationalize and understand these laws. This points to the existence of a rational, intelligent mind at the center responsible for its creation.

He reasons that our understanding of complex scientific theories goes beyond day-to-day tasks that we need to survive, and cannot be explained by the 'survival of the fittest' theory.

Secondly, Polkinghorne proposes reasoning that hinges on the anthropic principle — that the universe is balanced in a delicate, perfect balance of constants, such as gravity, that sustains life. Polkinghorne specifically cites the competing expanding force of the Big Bang and the contracting force of gravity. He explains that if the strength of gravity differed by one part in $10^{60}$, the universe would either expand too quickly and become too dilute for anything interesting to happen, or conversely, contract back and crash too quickly.

---

### Page 12

Polkinghorne takes this balance of settings, or "fine-tuning" of cosmic knobs, and describes it as a cosmic setting itself. He makes the assumption that a creator God is behind it. While Polkinghorne accepts attempts to justify the multiverse theory, he reasons that the multiverse is unsustainable and untestable, and that a creator God who wills into life a universe capable of producing beings who can come to know Him is much more likely.

Polkinghorne provides a hypothesis of complementarity of science and religion using scientific questions such as the origins of life and fine-tuning, which is still able to answer questions of meaning as Pope Benedict XVI describes.

However, Sam Harris illustrates a flaw in Polkinghorne's hypothesis, describing it as an unjustified leap of faith to presume that this scientific link to an unidentifiable deity is the God of Islam, Buddhism, or Christianity, or any religion.

### Page 13

The phenomenon proposed by Polkinghorne, Deism, conflicts with the ideas of dogma. The ability of Deism to answer the profound question of the meaning of life, as highlighted by Benedict XVI, is seemingly not enough to corroborate the arguments proposed by Durkheim, Gould, and Lennox over morality and ethics.

Therefore, complementarity could only be achieved by a leap of faith to adapt and use natural theology to justify religious beliefs. In this sense, while Polkinghorne's arguments are not necessarily invalid, the willingness to simply provide complementarity between science and religion via Deism is simply a hard caveat to move past.

Still, there is a third method of interaction between science and religion: conflict. The conflict between science and religion over the intervention of divine method can be overcome, however. Although, this method differs from the previous two described, as this time science must be the one willing to adapt.

Divine intervention is a foundational belief of religion, but it provides a difficult obstacle to overcome with science, as it contradicts the repeatable, empirical testing of consistent phenomena on which science is based.

The 18th-century physicist Isaac Newton expresses that science is "pleased by simplicity and nature is no dummy." Science is defined by reason and empirical evidence. Miracles, on the other hand, are defined by David Hume as violations of the laws of nature and are a difficult phenomenon for science to accept. They introduce unrepeatable, inconsistent anomalies into the framework of science.

Miracles, though, provide reasons for religion and provide answers to the profound questions of human existence, such as the meaning of life, that Benedict XVI highlights as the goal of religion.

For example, Christ does not simply hold value because he is an archetype, but because he is an interesting figure whose value is rooted in human suffering and his true testimony of history. It is the fact that his miracles are rooted in human suffering that gives Christianity its value.

### Page 15

The contemporary New Testament scholar [N.T. Wright] reflects this sentiment in reference to the resurrection, pointing out that *"the point of the resurrection is that the present body is not valueless just because it will die, but because what you do on earth matters because of the great future that God has in store for it."*

Though a willingness to redefine the foundational stone of science and instead provide complementarity could alleviate this conflict, David Hume, alongside his description of miracles, also proposed a Humean view of science.

The current view of science is a necessitarian view that argues the laws of science are absolute and we are able to predict phenomena for eternity. The Humean view, however, proposes that science cannot know for sure that any rule is eternally repeatable.

---

### Page 16

Instead, it only appears to be so based on previous observation. The Humean view proposes a scenario where miracles are not a violation of science, but exceptions to rules based on observation. This illustrates a viable scenario for complementarity between science and religion based on an openness to a Humean view of science.

This alternative view of science is not only caused by miracles, but could also allow for a creator as described by Polkinghorne, and for Durkheim and Gould's arguments for the validity of religion as a source of morality and ethics.

Together, the combination of the three could provide a holistic, scientific worldview where there are answers to the profound questions of humanity as described by Benedict XVI, while not discounting religious beliefs.

Rather, this assumption merely cannot say what happens in the future, as we cannot predict it based solely on previous scientific observations.

Presenting a definition of religion as per Durkheim's viewpoint, so too will a sense of morality disappear.

The complementarity between science and religion in this debate has been primarily hindered by the reluctance to accept alternative viewpoints that do not align with their beliefs. Though, the only way to find absolute complementarity is through the acceptance of caveats.

Gould's NOMA sees religion merely as a social construct, Deism as an answer to the creation of the universe. Each of which has a set of consequences to accept.
