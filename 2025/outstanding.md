$\text{\rlap{$\checkmark$}}\square$ **Question One**

N.P. = means paragraph

As Frantz Fanon contends in *The Wretched of the Earth*, "the Church in the colonies is the white people's church... She does not call the Native to God's way, but the way of the white man." This protests the colonial hierarchies, as Catholicism became complicit in European conquest, with colonialism carried out through the church rather than merely a byproduct. And yet, for Elizabeth Schüssler Fiorenza, Christianity's foundational vision holds an unthinkable urge for social justice and liberation.

Tom Holland argues that Christianity invented the moral grammar of Western society, championed the dignity of the weak, the poor, the foreigner. Yet, as spent centuries betrayal of this very values it claims to champion. This paradox captures the complex relationship between the Catholic Tradition and diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI). Still although Paul declared "There is neither Jew nor Greek, slave nor free, male or female" (Galatians 3:28), the Catholic Church has systematically excluded women from leadership betraying their founding vision.

To interrogate the nature of the dispute between Catholic tradition with diversity, equity, and inclusion is to ask whether it has succeeded or failed in meeting these demands, but to recognize that the principles of DEI constitute the same yardstick by which its responses are judged. At its simplest, Catholicism's most poignant claim is that humanity is created *imago Dei*, the image of God (Genesis 1:27).

---

### Page 4

From this axiom lies a moral revolution that human dignity is bestowed universally. If every human soul mirrors divine manifestation, then the differences of the species are sacramental rather than a threat. In practice lies the seeds for what modern discussion would call: diversity, equity, and inclusion. Here, an anthropological vision emerges where diversity is not obscene, nor a threat but sacramental, equity not a concession but justice, and inclusion not optional but essential.

Of the first principle, Catholic tradition has a great tapestry of diversity: "a great multitude from every tribe, tongue, people, and nation gathered in worship" (Rev 7:9). Today, over one billion Catholics worldwide embody a richness of cultures, languages, traditions, and gifts. Pope Francis affirms that "diversity is a richness, as never a reason for exclusion or discrimination." Meanwhile, through inculturation, Vatican II urges the Church to embrace the culture of a people while bringing the Gospel into it.

In Aotearoa New Zealand, Catholic schools and parishes have increasingly incorporated te reo Māori and tikanga into liturgy and classroom settings, while the New Zealand Catholic Bishops' Conference formally recognized Te Tiriti o Waitangi as integral to the Catholic mission.

### Page 5

N.P. Yet, Fanon's critique in *The Wretched of the Earth* remains potent. The Church in the colonies is the "white people's church ... She does not call the Native to God's way, but the way of the white man." The Doctrine of Discovery authorized Catholic imperial powers to dismantle indigenous spiritual systems and social structures.

In Aotearoa, missionary activities simultaneously carried the Gospel, but bound it to European cultural norms. This starkly contradicts the Gospel's innermost urge to embrace all people's gifts. And therefore, diversity, in this sense, is purely framed through the lens of European universality. The call for universal diversity and sharing of the Gospel is thereby proclaimed as a catalyst for global belonging, but in reality acts as a mask for cultural homogenization.

This epitomizes the paradox of Catholic operations of spreading the Gospel, whilst being accomplices of colonization and subsequent forced assimilation, causing difficult disputes over Catholicism's true allowance of cultural terms.

Shifting to equity, the principle itself extends beyond equality: demanding that systems themselves be reformed so all may flourish. Scripture insists that "God shows no partiality" (Acts 10:34), while James condemns favoritism towards the wealthy (James 2:1-7). Catholic Social Teaching echoes this, from *Rerum Novarum* (1891) to *Fratelli Tutti* (2020), emphasizing solidarity, rights of workers, and the preferential option for the poor. Caritas actively advocates for refugee support and hospitals around the world as equity in action.

---

### Page 6

In Aotearoa, parishes funding living wage, migrant resettlement, foodbanks, and shelters are embodiments of equity in action.

Yet, Elsa Tamez interprets James as indicting Christian communities for causing economic injustice. Disparities between dioceses, clerical privilege, wealth, and protection of institutional power all undercut equity.

In Aotearoa, the Royal Commission of Inquiry into Abuse in Care uncovered significant neglect in Catholic orphanages and schools, where voices of the vulnerable were suppressed to protect institutional reputation. Such failures directly clash with Jesus' warning that "if somebody causes one of these little ones to stumble, it would be better for them to have a millstone hung around their neck" (Matthew 18:6).

This dichotomy of best-value advocacy of equity against internal suppression of young causes intensifies the dispute between Catholicism and the principles of DEI. Equity is proclaimed in doctrine, but contested in practice — a response driven often only after scandal.

### Page 7

N.P. Moving to inclusion, the Catholic tradition defines itself as inclusive; indeed, *catholicus* means "according to the whole." Paul's vision of a body without division (Gal 3:28) grounds the Church's rhetoric that its inclusion is indeed universal. In *Fratelli Tutti*, Pope Francis rearticulates this calling for a "neighbour without exception."

In Aotearoa New Zealand, Catholic schools often lead pastoral care initiatives targeted for foreign, LGBTQ+, and indigenous students, enabled by a twenty-first-century regimen of inclusion. Meanwhile, integration of tikanga has to be considered alongside structural settings, including Britomart station's rebrand to Waitematā, which speaks to an adapting embrace of inclusion.

And yet in 2025, inclusion remains deeply contested in practice. The Vatican continues to bar women from ordination (*Ordinatio Sacerdotalis*, 1994), restricting their participation in sacramental leadership. Catechism still considers homosexual relationships "intrinsically disordered" (CCC 2357), while claiming the Church "cannot bless sin," excluding LGBTQ+ Catholics from full inclusion.

Catholic theologian James Alison critiques this language as merely inadequate, perpetuating a destructive exclusion. Likewise, the Catholic Church's interfaith relations share similar tensions: while *Nostra Aetate* (1965) affirmed interfaith dialogue, *Lumen Gentium* (1964) still claimed that the Church "possesses the fullness of truth." This dismissal of every other religion as "not fully true" is inherently exclusive, leaving pluralism conditional and its doctrines rigid.

---

### Page 8

Thus, inclusion in Catholic tradition is proclaimed universally, yet practiced selectively — visionary, but always hedged by conditions.

Therefore, to statement that there is a "dispute" between Catholicism and the principles of DEI is a relatively vindicated analysis. Analysis of doctrine and practice unveils a consistent level of hypocrisy between proclamations of Catholicism to acknowledge and traditions of practice. Thereby, this dichotomy would infer and contest that there is a conflict between principle and practice.

However, this conclusion fails to recognize the underlying product of this relationship. To ask if Catholicism has succeeded or failed the demands of diversity, equity, and inclusion is less a question of whether it has succeeded or failed, but more of a restless struggle. Its failures — exclusion of women, complicity in colonization, and suppression of abuse victims — are all real and egregious.

Yet, it is these failures themselves that hold the seeds for response and reform. Throughout this essay I have sprinkled elements of response to the Church's past association with colonial domination, which has sparked surges of inculturation (i.e. Waitematā Station). Its prior exclusion of women has birthed liberation theology and feminine theologies, while forcing the inclusion of LGBTQ+ Catholics to become a more poignant topic within the Vatican.

### Page 9

The Church's previous affiliation with abuse hegemony has motivated pastoral reforms, truth-tellings, and safeguarding policies in New Zealand. Failures in Māori engagement drive new partnerships formed by Te Kōhanga Reo and diverse accessible student groups in Catholic schools.

In post-crises, the contradictions of Catholic tradition do not mark complete failures, but testament to the faith's vitality: ever-adapting in joining the Gospel's doctrine with modern standards framed by diversity, equity, and accountability.

In reference to accountability, does the Catholic Tradition claim its shortcomings to respond? If the Church believed itself impervious, it would be in severe ignorance of Gospel text and writings. Instead, it acknowledges criteria for response that should be applicable to modern society.

The assemblies of the Church — including the Council of Trent, Second Vatican Council, and papal encyclicals — exist to make adaptations where necessary to doctrines established thousands of years ago, highlighting that their founding doctrines can be literally enforced today.

N.P. While Catholicism speaks to the world's majority faith, it is important to note that Eastern Orthodox Churches share similar struggles with gender equity and sexuality. Judaism and Islam likewise affirm human dignity as "mankind created in His image" (Genesis 1:27, Qur'an 95:4), but each religion faces disputes of diversity, equity, and inclusion under their respective doctrines.

### Page 10

What sets Catholicism apart is the sheer magnitude of its reach and scale of visibility — meaning its failures are both harder to ignore, but its responses are far more impactful.

The nature of the dispute of diversity, equity, and inclusion within Religious Tradition is therefore one of both dismissal and contradiction. However, to dismiss these religious traditions as a simple story of failure is to miss the crux of religious transformation. The power of religion is its ability to reshape itself under both the direction of its doctrine and the standards of modern society.

Catholicism's credibility lies not in pretending it has achieved perfect diversity, equity, and inclusion, but in its willingness to undergo continual conversion. As Pope Francis reminds us, "Let us not listen to standard uncertainties, let us not remain barricaded."

Diversity, equity, and inclusion are not modern measures designed to destroy the Church, but standard measures to measure the fidelity of religion to its own Gospel — a standard which the Church will continue to strive, fail, and strive again to meet.
