**Comparability flags**

- **Mark status.** Only seven essays carry a mark. "Total score: 24" in several headers is the paper maximum, not a mark. 23-T, 24-T, 25-T, 25-O and 25-S are banded by label or filename only. 25-Jo, 25-Je and 25-Sa have no label, mark, question page or planning page: I use them as contrast, never as evidence of what scores.
- **Transcription noise.** 22-S and 22-O contain \[?\]/\[unclear\]; 25-T contains struck words; 24-T has some broken sentences (p.20: "Exegesis and empiricism are tokened by your views"). I build no claim on illegible text.
- **Genre.** 2022–24 are continuous prose. Four of six 2025 essays (25-T, 25-S, 25-Jo, 25-Sa) are sectioned, because the 2025 prompt names three terms. Approaches differ: lens-driven argument (24-T, 22-T, 25-Je), a case set tested on one criterion (22-O, 24-O), and survey-of-scholars (23-O, 24-S, 25-S, 25-Sa). These are not interchangeable models.
- **Sample bias.** Every essay centres Western Christianity (mostly Catholic) except 22-O's Andal block. Nothing here shows how a non-Christian-centred essay would fare.
- **Length is not a proxy.** 22-S (13) and 22-O (22) are about the same length; 25-O (≈1,800) is filed Outstanding; 25-S (≈4,900) is not.

**The Standard as yardstick.** The Standard requires analysis and critical thinking ("examination of key assumptions and alternative points of view"); integration and synthesis ("forming relationships and connections between religious ideas"); and logical development with "accurate, non-pejorative and consistent use of language". Outstanding adds perception and insight, "independent reflection and extrapolation" ("seeing application and implication beyond an immediate context") and "originality and thorough exploration". Marker commentary echoes this wording (24-O Aspect 1: "perceptive and insightful comprehension"; 23-O: implications "beyond the immediate context"). The only direct explanations of lower marks converge on one cluster: 22-S, "Greater clarity and engagement with counter arguments"; 22-O, "more ... counter examples"; 23-S, "more specific studies of psychology"; 24-S, "important points were missing, along with an inadequately defended generalisation". Structure matters below only insofar as it delivers these.

**Introductions** take four forms. (a) A qualified thesis: 24-O p.6, "a willingness to accept caveats ... can illustrate complementarity between the two, although this is a hard task". (b) A direct position: 25-Jo, "Christianity and DEI in its current secular interpretation are incompatible". (c) A question-led reframe: 25-O p.3, "to ask whether it has succeeded or failed ... but to recognize that the principles of DEI constitute the same yardstick by which its responses are judged". (d) A frame with no stake: 23-S ¶1, "confused, enlightened, angered and calmed many researchers". 25-S defers its judgement entirely to its last section ("While I do not feel I can call for the Church to ordain women without undermining its firm tradition, how we define leadership ... is much more of a grey area"), which is defensible but leaves the reader without a thesis for four pages.

**Organising logic** is mixed. Dialectical: 22-T; 25-O ("acted" versus "contradicted" for each term, planned on its p.2). Lens-driven cases: 24-T (Kuhn applied to Galileo, Al-Ghazali, creationism); 25-Je (Newman's tests). Problem–solution chain: 24-O (each solution fails on a caveat that motivates the next). One criterion applied to a case set: 22-O. Survey of positions: 23-O, 24-S, 25-S, 25-Sa. No essay is chronological throughout.

**Managing competing perspectives** has three modes. (1) Paired objection, no adjudication: 24-S sets Dawkins against Plantinga, Sagan against Craig, Collins against Harris, each objection one sentence, and nothing later depends on it. (2) Objection chain with concession: 24-O p.10, "this complimentary attempt by Gould, Durkheim, Lennox, and Hick can only be achieved under a willingness to allow religion to be reduced to a social construct, which is unsustainable." (3) Adjudication by criterion: 23-T ¶10, "Whilst the perennialist approach seems to ignore the very plausible cultural influence ... the constructivists tend to ignore the undeniable 'felt' sensations", followed by a verdict on what survives.

**Most effective transitions**

1. 24-O p.10: "Is there, therefore, a way for the profound answering ability explained by Benedict to be kept while still offering complementarity between science and religion?" It names exactly what the previous section lost and sets the next section's job.
2. 25-T pp.8–9: "The great assumption behind the entirety of Section A)" ... "If this identity is simply... untrue, then the principles of religion promoting social justice fail to hold." It converts the last section's premise into the next section's test, which is the Standard's "key assumptions" in action.
3. 22-T ¶13: "Overall, then, we have a thesis and an antithesis, both with compelling (and seemingly contradictory) cases." It states the tension to be resolved before resolving it.
4. 24-T p.10: "So in this case it's not questioning that is difficult, but the answering." It turns a narrated case into the thesis's key distinction.
5. 24-O p.13: "this method differs from the previous two described, as this time science must be the one willing to adapt." It states what changes between sections.

By contrast, "This brings us to Section B)" (25-T p.8) works only because the preceding sentence carries the content: "the dispute ... is rooted in different beliefs around why social justice should be achieved".

## 2. Paragraph mechanics

The move sequence claim → evidence → interpretation → counter-view → adjudication → link is **not corpus-wide**. It is regular in 22-O, 22-T, 23-T, 24-O, 24-T and 25-O, and truncated (claim + illustration, or description + "However") in 24-S, 23-S, 22-S and 25-Sa.

**Evidence-to-commentary ratio.** Higher-marked or top-filed scripts spend about half or more of each block on interpretation and adjudication (22-O, 24-T, 23-T). 24-S and 25-Sa spend most of theirs on summary. 25-T is the exception: dense in evidence, light in commentary, filed Top Scholar. I do not infer marks from that, but it shows that a script can carry heavy quotation if its section-level argument is strong.

**Terminology.** Precise and reused: 22-O's "dependent variable"; 23-T's "religiousness" (defined via Paloutzian, then used in the thesis and conclusion); 25-T's "soft equity" versus "hard equity". Vague through repetition: 24-S uses "constructive dialogue" at least eight times as a label with no specification.

## 3. What distinguishes stronger execution

1. **Operationalising the question's key term.** 22-O turns "significant progress" into a test applied to every case. 22-T splits the question: "Has any significant progress been made in empowering women?" versus whether women have "a more equal footing", and answers "No such progress beyond the superficial has been made within the church" / "a partial positive". 23-O's planning table assigns each psychologist "WEAKNESSES/NOT USEFUL" versus "USEFUL/CONVINCING" and sorts audiences (everyone, atheists, religious) to answer "For whom, and to what extent".
2. **A framework that yields a judgement per case.** 24-T's Kuhn produces a different finding each time: Galileo ("the paradigm does not falsify itself"), Al-Ghazali, creationism ("retroactive justification of pre-existing beliefs"). Counter-example: 24-S announces Barbour's taxonomy (p.5), lists four models (p.6), then never classifies a case; even the Scopes trial, the standard conflict-model case, is resolved with "constructive dialogue".
3. **Controlled comparison** (individual technique). 24-T pairs a failing case with a succeeding one to isolate the variable: "Al-Ghazali had no such constraints, and his contention of the Falsafa paradigm was far more of a rational argument" (p.12), then names two conditions: institutional support for testing, and no "dogmatic adherence to extra-scriptural, contingent lines" (p.13).
4. **Naming the assumption behind an argument.** 23-T ¶13: "The assumption that this argument makes however is that all humans desire spirituality and religion to achieve a sense of unity." 25-T p.8: "The great assumption behind the entirety of Section A)". Rare in 24-S and 22-S, though 22-S has one good instance of it (p.11): "would that not mean that women were already speaking in Churches?"
5. **A two-part verdict.** 22-O (groundwork versus significant progress); 22-T (A versus B); 25-O p.8, where "success or failure" is replaced by "a restless struggle", planned on its p.2 as "fidelity ... vs ever-changing standards".
6. **Extrapolation beyond the immediate context** (Outstanding descriptor). 24-T p.19: "Oil companies with paradigmatic biases proclaim the lack of danger in their actions for the climate." 22-O p.11: "this shift has been overwhelmingly societal, not religious" (planning note: "Talk about non-religious"). 24-O p.17 projects the loss of morality if religion declines. Over-extension is a risk: 23-T ¶16 ends "Perhaps with further conclusive evidence, the future may hold an end to religious conflicts".
7. **Empathetic representation before critique** ("perception and insight"). 22-T lays out Aquinas, Augustine and Luther on submission before distinguishing "submission" from "oppression"; 24-T gives Al-Ghazali's hermeneutic its own terms.

## 5. Reusable writing blueprint (conditional, not universal)

**Introduction**

1. Anchor on the question's pivot term. Quote a stimulus only if you will analyse it (23-T, 22-T).
2. Define one or two terms you will reuse as tests (22-O, 23-T). Skip dictionary definitions.
3. Fix scope in one sentence (tradition, period).
4. State a qualified thesis: claim plus its limit (24-T, 22-O, 25-O). Alternative: a question-led reframe (25-O).
5. Roadmap only if you have more than three blocks; otherwise signpost as you go (24-T, 25-O).

**Body block (4–6 moves; not every block needs all)**

1. Claim tied to the thesis or key term.
2. Evidence: primary text or named scholar, short exact quotation, location.
3. Interpretation in your own words under your lens.
4. Counter-view: a named critic or rival reading.
5. Adjudicate: say which holds, on what criterion, and what survives.
6. Link: apply the key term to the question (22-O), then set up the next block with the gap it leaves (24-O).

**Counter-view handling.** Place the objection where it bites. Give it one sentence of exposition and one of adjudication (23-T ¶10). Protect the thesis by narrowing the claim rather than dropping it (24-O: "this definition ... comes with a caveat"). If you have a failing and a succeeding case, compare them (24-T).

**Synthesis.** Separate sub-claims so each can have its own verdict (22-O). Show the relationship between sections, not only their sequence (24-O p.16, "Together, the combination of the three ..."; 22-T's complementarianism as synthesis). Include one implication beyond the immediate context.

**Conclusion.** Answer in two or three sentences with the qualification built in. State what your evidence cannot show (24-T p.6: "this does not claim them to be false"). Introduce no new frame (25-T). If you use a closing quotation, put it after the judgement (24-O's Bacon follows its Humean verdict on p.18).

**Applying this to your theme (spiritual traditions and war, Catholic focus).** Treat the Thomist–Augustinian framework as a *test* applied to cases, as 22-O does with "significant progress" and 25-Je does with Newman's tests, not as a label: for example Aquinas's three conditions (ST II-II q.40 a.1: authority, just cause, right intention) with Augustine's peace-ordered account, run against two or three cases, each with a named objection and a qualification. Use Thomistic metaphysics only where it does argumentative work.

## Council review

- **Contrarian:** n = 15; seven marks; three unmarked scripts; the conclusions are associations. The most reliable evidence is the marker commentary above, not my style readings.
- **First Principles:** The Standard rewards examined assumptions, relations between ideas, and extrapolation. Structure (triads, roadmaps) is incidental, and the corpus confirms it: roadmaps and triads occur on both sides of the band line.
- **Expansionist:** Controlled comparison (24-T) and the key-term test (22-O) transfer to any theme.
- **Outsider:** Terms such as "Hegelian dialectic" (22-T) and "paradigm" (24-T) are opaque unless used and defined in the essay itself.
- **Executor:** Write one six-move block under time pressure and compare it with the 22-O Mary paragraph.

**Chairman's verdict.** In the marked scripts, the differentiator is not structure but whether the essay adjudicates a counter-view against a stated criterion, answers every part of the question, and extrapolates. **First thing to do:** write one timed block on a past question in the six-move form.

## Checklists

**A. Knowledge required before the exam**

- Definitions of the theme's key terms, attributed to scholars, in a form you can use as a test.
- Two or three primary texts with exact short quotations and references.
- Three or four named scholars, each with a position and the best named objection.
- One lens you can apply repeatedly across 2–3 cases, with the verdict it produces for each.
- Two or three historical or modern cases with dates, titles and attributions checked (the corpus slips are almost all here).
- The strongest counter-reading to your own thesis.
- One implication beyond the immediate context.
- For your theme: Aquinas ST II-II q.40; Augustine on just war and peace (*Contra Faustum* 22.74–76); CCC 2308–2310; *Gaudium et Spes* 79–82; Francis, *Fratelli Tutti* 258; a pacifist or Gospel-nonviolence objection.

**B. In-exam reminders**

1. Mark the pivot term and every part of both questions (been/could be; accountability).
2. Plan: a thesis with a limit, and a table mapping question terms to blocks.
3. Define only what you will reuse.
4. Each block: claim → evidence → interpretation → counter → adjudication → link.
5. Name at least one assumption.
6. Check every quotation: exact, attributed, and doing work.
7. Use neutral language ("Catholic teaching holds", not "invented belief").
8. Leave 15 minutes for a conclusion that gives a verdict, introduces no new frame and does not end on a quotation.
9. If unsure of a date or attribution, omit it.
